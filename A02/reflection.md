# Reflection — P+7 and P+x

My script gives GPT-2 *The Snow Man* line by line, hides the last word, and replaces it with the word at rank x in the model's predictions. I only counted complete English words, because the raw rankings are crowded with commas, line breaks and fragments such as "th" or "gro".

Low values of x barely disturb the poem. At P+2 and P+3 GPT-2 suggests "snow", "rain", "forest" and "a long while", so it stays inside the same winter vocabulary that Stevens already set up. The model's favourite words are predictable and generic, and the poem becomes a blander version of itself. P+7 is where the strangeness starts: "pine-trees crusted with blood" and "junipers shagged with blood" turn a quiet landscape into a horror scene, while the line "a long long" shows that a mechanically chosen word can break grammar.

As x rises, the replacements move further from both the winter theme and grammatical sense. I chose P+250 because it is still readable but absurd: "have been cold a long cup", "the sound of the yell" and "the listener, who listens in the melancholy" sound almost meaningful, which makes them funnier. At P+500 the words become near-random ("the frost and the par") and the joke disappears, because nothing is left to contrast with the original. The comedy lives in the middle range, where the word is unexpected but still feels like it could belong.

To replace every noun with its seventh-highest alternative, I would tag the poem with spaCy and loop over each token labelled NOUN. For each noun, I would give GPT-2 the text before it, walk down its ranked predictions, and keep only candidates that spaCy also tags as a noun in that position, taking the seventh. This keeps N+7's "maintain grammar" rule. My notebook includes a rough version that produced lines such as "Of any tree in the earth of the voice". Its main limitation is that GPT-2 only sees the words to the left, so a masked model like BERT, which reads both sides of the blank, might choose better-fitting nouns.
